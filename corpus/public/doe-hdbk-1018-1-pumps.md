## Centrifugal Pumps

Centrifugal pumps are the most common type of pump found in DOE facilities. They enjoy widespread application partly due to their ability to operate over a wide range of flow rates and pump heads.

Centrifugal pumps basically consist of a stationary pump casing and an impeller mounted on a rotating shaft. The pump casing provides a pressure boundary for the pump and contains channels to properly direct the suction and discharge flow, with suction and discharge penetrations for the main flow path and normally small drain and vent fittings to remove trapped gases or drain the casing for maintenance.

The pump casing guides the liquid from the suction connection to the center, or eye, of the impeller. The vanes of the rotating impeller impart a radial and rotary motion to the liquid, forcing it to the outer periphery of the pump casing where it is collected in the outer part of the casing called the volute. The volute expands in cross-sectional area as it wraps around the pump casing, collecting liquid discharged from the periphery of the impeller at high velocity and gradually reducing fluid velocity by increasing the flow area — converting velocity head to static pressure. The fluid is then discharged from the pump through the discharge connection.

Some centrifugal pumps are constructed with two distinct volutes, each receiving liquid discharged from a 180° region of the impeller at any given time. Pumps of this type are called double volute (or split volute) pumps; in some applications the double volute minimizes radial forces imparted to the shaft and bearings due to imbalances in the pressure around the impeller.

### Diffuser

Some centrifugal pumps contain diffusers. A diffuser is a set of stationary vanes that surround the impeller. Its purpose is to increase pump efficiency by allowing a more gradual expansion and less turbulent area for the liquid to reduce in velocity. The diffuser vanes are designed so the liquid exiting the impeller encounters an ever-increasing flow area as it passes through the diffuser, reducing flow velocity and converting kinetic energy into flow pressure.

### Impeller Classification

Impellers are classified based on the number of points at which liquid can enter the impeller, and on the amount of webbing between the impeller blades.

Impellers can be single-suction (liquid enters the center of the blades from only one direction) or double-suction (liquid enters the center of the impeller blades from both sides simultaneously).

Impellers can also be open (blades attached only to a hub), semi-open (a circular web plate attached to one side of the blades), or enclosed/shrouded (circular plates attached to both sides of the blades). The impeller sometimes contains balancing holes connecting the space around the hub to the suction side of the impeller; these holes have a total cross-sectional area considerably greater than the annular space between the wearing ring and the hub, resulting in suction pressure on both sides of the impeller hub and maintaining hydraulic balance of axial thrust.

### Centrifugal Pump Classification by Flow

Centrifugal pumps can be classified by the manner in which fluid flows through the pump, determined by the design of the pump casing and impeller:

- **Radial flow pumps** — liquid enters at the center of the impeller and is directed out along the impeller blades at right angles to the pump shaft.
- **Axial flow pumps** — the impeller pushes liquid in a direction parallel to the pump shaft; sometimes called propeller pumps because they operate essentially like a boat propeller.
- **Mixed flow pumps** — borrow characteristics from both radial and axial flow pumps; the impeller blades push liquid out away from the pump shaft and toward the pump suction at an angle greater than 90°.

### Multi-Stage Centrifugal Pumps

A centrifugal pump with a single impeller that can develop a differential pressure of more than 150 psid between suction and discharge is difficult and costly to design and construct. A more economical approach to developing high pressures with a single centrifugal pump is to include multiple impellers on a common shaft within the same pump casing — internal channels route the discharge of one impeller to the suction of the next.

A pump stage is that portion of a centrifugal pump consisting of one impeller and its associated components. Most centrifugal pumps are single-stage; a pump containing seven impellers within a single casing would be a seven-stage pump, or generally, a multi-stage pump.

### Centrifugal Pump Components

Centrifugal pumps vary in design from simple pumps with relatively few parts to extremely complicated pumps with hundreds of individual parts. Common components include wearing rings, stuffing boxes, packing, and lantern rings.

- **Wearing rings** — Small clearances are maintained between the rotating impeller and stationary pump casing so the impeller can rotate freely; wear/erosion occurs at this near-contact point over time, increasing leakage. Wearing rings are replaceable rings attached to the impeller and/or pump casing that allow a small running clearance without wearing the actual impeller or casing material, and are designed to be replaced periodically to avoid the more costly replacement of the impeller or casing.
- **Stuffing box** — Where the rotating shaft penetrates the pump casing's pressure boundary, a stuffing box (a cylindrical space surrounding the shaft) holds rings of packing material that form a seal controlling leakage along the shaft. Packing rings are held in place by a gland, itself held by studs with adjusting nuts; tightening the nuts compresses the packing axially, causing it to expand radially and form a tight seal. Stuffing boxes are normally designed to allow a small amount of controlled leakage along the shaft to lubricate and cool the packing; the leakage rate is adjusted by tightening/loosening the packing gland.
- **Lantern ring** — Used when a standard stuffing box cannot adequately seal or cool the shaft (e.g., suction under vacuum, or fluid too hot). A lantern ring is a perforated hollow ring located near the center of the packing box that receives relatively cool, clean liquid (from the pump discharge or an external source) and distributes it uniformly around the shaft to cool and lubricate the packing, or to seal against air leakage into the pump if suction pressure is below atmospheric.
- **Mechanical seals** — An alternative to packing in some situations, consisting of a rotating element attached to the pump shaft and a stationary element attached to the pump casing, each with a highly polished sealing surface; the polished faces contact each other to form a seal preventing leakage along the shaft.

**Summary:** The impeller imparts radial and rotary motion to the liquid; the volute converts velocity head to static head; a diffuser (where present) increases efficiency via gradual expansion; packing seals the shaft penetration; wearing rings allow a small running clearance without wearing the impeller/casing; the lantern ring provides cooling/lubricating liquid to the packing.

## Centrifugal Pump Operation

Improper operation of centrifugal pumps can damage the pump and cause loss of function of the system it serves. Many centrifugal pumps are designed to operate continuously for months or years, relying on the liquid being pumped to cool and lubricate bearings and other internal components — if flow through the pump stops while it continues operating, the pump is no longer adequately cooled and can quickly become damaged. Damage can also result from pumping liquid whose temperature is close to saturated conditions.

### Cavitation

The flow area at the eye of the pump impeller is usually smaller than the flow area of the suction piping or through the impeller vanes. As liquid enters the eye, the decrease in flow area increases flow velocity and decreases pressure. If the pressure drop is large enough (or temperature high enough), the liquid can flash to vapor when local pressure falls below the saturation pressure for the fluid. Vapor bubbles formed at the eye of the impeller are swept along the impeller vanes; when they reach a region of higher pressure farther out the vane, they abruptly collapse. This formation-and-collapse process is called **cavitation**.

Cavitation degrades pump performance (fluctuating flow rate and discharge pressure) and can be destructive: vapor bubbles collapsing near the leading edge of impeller vanes create microscopic pits whose cumulative effect over hours or days can destroy an impeller, and cavitation can cause excessive vibration that damages bearings, wearing rings, and seals. A cavitating pump can sound like a can of marbles being shaken; other remote indications include fluctuating discharge pressure, flow rate, and pump motor current.

### Net Positive Suction Head

To avoid cavitation, the pressure of the fluid at all points within the pump must remain above saturation pressure. The **net positive suction head available (NPSHA)** is the difference between the pressure at the pump suction and the saturation pressure for the liquid being pumped. The **net positive suction head required (NPSHR)** is the minimum net positive suction head necessary to avoid cavitation. To avoid cavitation:

```
NPSHA ≥ NPSHR
NPSHA = P_suction − P_saturation
NPSHA = Pa + Pst − hf − Psat
```

where `Pa` = absolute pressure on the surface of the liquid, `Pst` = pressure due to elevation between liquid surface and pump suction, `hf` = head losses in the suction piping, and `Psat` = saturation pressure of the liquid being pumped.

### Preventing Cavitation

Several changes can increase NPSHA above NPSHR to stop cavitation: increasing pressure at the pump suction (e.g., raising liquid level in an enclosed tank, or increasing pressure above the liquid); decreasing the temperature of the liquid being pumped (which decreases saturation pressure); reducing head losses in the suction piping (larger pipe diameter, fewer elbows/valves/fittings, shorter pipe length). It may also be possible to reduce NPSHR itself — NPSHR typically increases as flow rate through the pump increases, so throttling a discharge valve to reduce flow decreases NPSHR; NPSHR also increases with pump speed, so reducing the speed of a variable-speed pump decreases NPSHR. NPSHR is determined through manufacturer testing and depends on impeller inlet type, impeller design, flow rate, rotational speed, and the liquid being pumped; manufacturers typically supply NPSHR-vs-flow-rate curves in the pump's vendor manual.

### Centrifugal Pump Characteristic Curves

For a pump operating at constant speed, flow rate depends on the differential pressure (head) developed by the pump — the lower the head, the higher the flow rate. A vendor manual usually contains a pump characteristic curve (flow rate vs. head). **Shutoff head** is the maximum head a centrifugal pump can develop at a set speed. **Pump runout** is the maximum flow a centrifugal pump can develop without being damaged. Pumps must be designed and operated to be protected from both conditions.

### Centrifugal Pump Protection

A centrifugal pump is **dead-headed** when operated with no flow through it (e.g., closed discharge valve, or against a seated check valve). The impeller churns the same volume of water, increasing its temperature (via friction) until it flashes to vapor, which can interrupt cooling flow to the packing and bearings and, if sustained, damage the pump. Protection is typically provided by a recirculation line from the pump discharge (upstream of the discharge valve) back to the pump's supply source, sized to allow enough flow to prevent overheating; automatic flow control devices may also be used.

Pumps must also be protected from **runout**, which can lead to cavitation and motor overheating from excessive current. One method is placing an orifice or throttle valve immediately downstream of the pump discharge to ensure adequate flow resistance; properly designed piping systems are important for runout protection.

### Gas Binding

Gas binding is a condition where the pump casing fills with gases or vapors to the point the impeller can no longer contact enough fluid to function — the impeller spins in the gas bubble without forcing liquid through the pump, which can cause cooling problems for packing and bearings. Most centrifugal pumps can tolerate a small amount of accumulated gas, but pumps in systems with dissolved gases (that are not self-venting) should be periodically vented manually.

### Priming Centrifugal Pumps

Most centrifugal pumps are not self-priming — the casing must be filled with liquid before starting, or the pump cannot function; if the casing fills with vapor/gas the impeller becomes gas-bound. To stay primed, most centrifugal pumps are located below the level of the liquid source they take suction from, or liquid is supplied to the suction under pressure from another pump in the suction line.

**Summary:** Indications of cavitation are noise, fluctuating discharge pressure/flow, and fluctuating motor current. Steps to stop cavitation: increase suction pressure, reduce liquid temperature, reduce suction piping head losses, reduce flow rate, reduce impeller speed. Effects of cavitation: degraded performance, excessive vibration, damage to impeller/bearings/wearing rings/seals. Pumps are protected from dead-heading via a recirculation line and from runout via an orifice or throttle valve plus proper piping design.

## Positive Displacement Pumps

Positive displacement pumps operate on a different principle than centrifugal pumps: they physically entrap a quantity of liquid at the suction and push that quantity out the discharge. A definite volume of liquid is delivered per cycle of pump operation, constant regardless of system flow resistance (provided power-unit capacity or component strength limits aren't exceeded) — this contrasts with centrifugal pumps, which deliver continuous flow for a given speed and discharge resistance. Positive displacement pumps fall into three basic categories: reciprocating pumps, rotary pumps, and diaphragm pumps.

### Principle of Operation

Demonstrated most simply by a reciprocating positive displacement pump with a single piston in a cylinder, one suction port, and one discharge port, with check valves in each port allowing flow in only one direction. During the suction stroke the piston moves to admit liquid from the reservoir (opening the suction check valve); during the discharge stroke the piston seats the suction check valve and opens the discharge check valve. The volume moved per cycle equals the change in cylinder liquid volume between the piston's extreme positions.

### Reciprocating Pumps

Categorized in four ways:
- **Direct-acting vs. indirect-acting** — direct-acting pumps have a plunger directly driven by the pump rod (an extension of a reciprocating power piston rod, e.g. a steam piston); indirect-acting pumps are driven via a beam or linkage connected to a separate reciprocating engine's power piston rod.
- **Simplex vs. duplex** — a simplex (single) pump has one liquid cylinder; a duplex pump is the equivalent of two simplex pumps side by side, with pistons driven so one is on its upstroke while the other is on its downstroke, doubling capacity versus a comparable simplex pump.
- **Single-acting vs. double-acting** — a single-acting pump fills the cylinder on the suction stroke and discharges on the return stroke; a double-acting pump fills one end of the cylinder while discharging from the other, reversing on the return stroke.
- **Power pumps** — convert rotary motion to low-speed reciprocating motion via reduction gearing, a crankshaft, connecting rods, and crossheads. Lower-pressure, higher-capacity units typically use duplex double-acting construction; higher-pressure units are normally single-acting plungers, often in sets of three (triplex) to substantially reduce flow pulsations. Power pumps have high efficiency and can develop very high pressures, driven by electric motors or turbines; they are relatively expensive and rarely justified over centrifugal pumps on efficiency alone, but are often justified over steam reciprocating pumps for continuous-duty service.

In general, the effective flow rate of reciprocating pumps decreases as fluid viscosity increases (because pump speed must be reduced). Unlike centrifugal pumps, the differential pressure generated by reciprocating pumps is independent of fluid density and depends entirely on the force exerted on the piston.

### Rotary Pumps

Rotary pumps use a rotating vane, screw, or gear to trap liquid at the suction side of the casing and force it to the discharge side. They are essentially self-priming (can remove air from suction lines and produce high suction lift). Close clearances between rotating and stationary parts are essential to minimize **slippage** — leakage of fluid from the discharge back to the suction — which is why these pumps are operated at relatively low speed to maintain capacity over time and avoid erosive wear that would increase clearances. Rotary pumps are grouped into three categories:

- **Gear pumps** — a simple gear pump uses two meshing spur gears revolving in opposite directions within a casing, with only a few thousandths of an inch clearance; liquid trapped between successive gear teeth and the case is carried around and forced out the discharge as the teeth mesh. Fewer teeth increases capacity but tends toward pulsating discharge. There are no valves (unlike reciprocating pumps) and no high impeller velocities (unlike centrifugal pumps), making gear pumps well suited to viscous fluids such as fuel and lubricating oils. Variants include helical gear pumps (steadier discharge, fewer pulsations than spur gears, since the engagement point moves along the tooth length) and herringbone gear pumps (two helixes spiraling in different directions, providing overlapping/steadier discharge and smoother, quieter power transmission).
- **Lobe-type pumps** — essentially a simple gear pump with only two or three teeth (lobes) per rotor; some designs use replaceable gibs (thin plates in grooves at each lobe's extremity) to promote tightness and absorb radial wear.
- **Screw-type pumps** — e.g. the two-screw, low-pitch design (two intermeshing screws, one right-handed and one left-handed thread, on parallel shafts, with a driving shaft turning the other through herringbone timing gears; liquid is trapped in spiral-shaped quantities and slides along the cylinder to a center discharge, with opposed streams eliminating hydraulic thrust) and the three-screw, high-pitch design (a center power rotor drives two outer idler rotors directly without external timing gears, used for pumping viscous fluids such as lubricating, hydraulic, or fuel oil).
- **Rotary moving vane pumps** — a cylindrically shaped rotor (smaller diameter than the housing bore) is driven off-center within a cylindrically bored housing; vanes move in and out of the rotor as it turns to maintain sealed spaces between rotor and cylinder wall, trapping liquid or gas on the suction side and expelling it through the discharge as the space contracts.

### Diaphragm Pumps

Classified as positive displacement pumps because the diaphragm acts as a limited-displacement piston, forced into reciprocating motion by mechanical linkage, compressed air, or pulsating fluid from an external source. Construction eliminates contact between the pumped liquid and the energy source, eliminating leakage — important for toxic or expensive liquids. Disadvantages include limited head/capacity range and the need for check valves in the suction and discharge nozzles.

### Positive Displacement Pump Characteristic Curves

Because a positive displacement pump delivers a fixed volume per cycle, the only factor affecting flow rate in an ideal pump is operating speed — system flow resistance does not affect flow rate. In practice, as discharge pressure increases, some liquid leaks from the discharge back to the suction (**slippage**), reducing effective flow rate — reflected as a curve deviating from the ideal vertical line on a head-vs-flow plot.

### Positive Displacement Pump Protection

Positive displacement pumps are normally fitted with relief valves upstream of their discharge valves to protect the pump and discharge piping from overpressurization — since these pumps discharge at whatever pressure the system demands, a relief valve prevents damage if the discharge valve is shut during operation or flow is otherwise blocked (e.g., a clogged strainer).

**Summary:** Positive displacement pumps are classified as reciprocating piston, gear-type rotary, lobe-type rotary, screw-type rotary, moving vane, or diaphragm pumps. As liquid viscosity increases, the maximum operating speed (and flow rate) of a reciprocating pump decreases. The characteristic curve at a given speed is a vertical line on a head-vs-flow graph. Slippage is the rate at which liquid leaks from discharge back to suction. Positive displacement pumps are protected from overpressurization by a relief valve upstream of the discharge valve.
