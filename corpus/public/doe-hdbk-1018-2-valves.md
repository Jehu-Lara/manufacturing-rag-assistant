## Valve Functions and Basic Parts

Valves are the most common single piece of equipment found in DOE facilities. A valve is a mechanical device that controls the flow and pressure of fluid within a system or process by performing one or more of the following functions: stopping and starting fluid flow, varying (throttling) the amount of fluid flow, controlling the direction of fluid flow, regulating downstream system or process pressure, and relieving component or piping overpressure. Regardless of type, all valves have the following basic parts: the body, bonnet, trim (internal elements), actuator, and packing.

### Valve Body

The body (sometimes called the shell) is the primary pressure boundary of a valve — the framework that holds everything together and resists fluid pressure loads from the connecting piping, receiving inlet and outlet piping through threaded, bolted, or welded joints. Valve bodies are cast or forged into a variety of shapes; while a sphere or cylinder would theoretically be the most economical shape to resist pressure, practical considerations (support for the seat/throttling orifice, load distortion from end connections, ease of manufacture and assembly, and cost) mean valve bodies typically range from simple block shapes to complex shapes in which a removable bonnet forms part of the pressure-resisting body.

### Valve Bonnet

The bonnet is the cover for the opening in the valve body and is the second principal pressure boundary — cast or forged of the same material as the body and connected to it by a threaded, bolted, or welded joint (the attachment itself is a pressure boundary). Some bonnets function simply as covers, while others support valve internals and accessories such as the stem, disk, and actuator. Bonnets are a necessity for most valves but a cause for concern: they can complicate manufacture, increase valve size, represent a significant cost portion, and are a potential source of leakage.

### Valve Trim

The internal elements of a valve are collectively called its trim, typically including a disk, seat, stem, and guide sleeves. A valve's performance is determined by the disk-and-seat interface and the relation of disk position to seat. In rotational-motion trim designs, the disk slides closely past the seat to change the flow opening; in linear-motion trim designs, the disk lifts perpendicularly away from the seat so an annular orifice appears.

- **Disk and seat** — For a valve with a bonnet, the disk is the third principal pressure boundary: with the disk closed, full system pressure is applied across it if the outlet side is depressurized, so the disk is a pressure-retaining part. Disks are typically forged and often hard-surfaced for wear resistance; a fine seating-area surface finish is necessary for good sealing. The seat (or seal rings) provides the seating surface for the disk — sometimes machined directly into the body, sometimes threaded or welded seal rings (often hard-faced) that are not usually considered pressure-boundary parts since the body wall alone withstands design pressure.
- **Stem** — Connects the actuator to the disk and positions it; typically forged and threaded or welded to the disk, not normally a pressure-boundary part. Valves use either a **rising stem** (the stem rises above the actuator as the valve opens, because it is threaded and mated to a yoke bushing on the bonnet) or a **nonrising stem** (no upward movement outside the valve; the disk itself is threaded internally and mates with the stem threads, keeping the stem threads within the packing boundary and out of contact with the environment).

### Valve Actuator

The actuator operates the stem-and-disk assembly and may be a manually operated handwheel or lever, a motor operator, a solenoid operator, a pneumatic operator, or a hydraulic ram. Except for certain hydraulically controlled valves, actuators (and any supporting yoke) are outside the pressure boundary.

### Valve Packing

Most valves use packing — commonly a fibrous material (such as flax) or a compound (such as Teflon) — to prevent leakage between the stem and bonnet. Packing must be properly compressed: too loose and the valve leaks (a safety hazard); too tight and it impairs stem movement or damages the stem.

### Introduction to the Types of Valves

Common valve types include the globe, gate, ball, plug, butterfly, diaphragm, check, pinch, and safety valve, each suited to particular needs — some throttle flow, others only stop it, some suit corrosive systems, others high-pressure fluids. There are four basic methods of controlling flow through a valve:

1. Move a disc or plug into or against an orifice (e.g., globe or needle valve).
2. Slide a flat, cylindrical, or spherical surface across an orifice (e.g., gate and plug valves).
3. Rotate a disc or ellipse about a shaft extending across the diameter of an orifice (e.g., butterfly or ball valve).
4. Move a flexible material into the flow passage (e.g., diaphragm and pinch valves).

## Types of Valves

### Gate Valves

A gate valve is a linear-motion valve used to start or stop flow — it does not regulate or throttle. The disk is completely removed from the flow stream when fully open, offering virtually no flow resistance and little pressure drop; when fully closed, 360° disk-to-seal-ring contact provides good sealing. Because the flow path opens in a highly nonlinear way with respect to percent-open, and a partially open disk tends to vibrate (with most flow change occurring near shutoff at high velocity, causing disk/seat wear), gate valves are not used to regulate or throttle flow. Disks are classified as solid wedge (most common; simple, strong, usable in any position), flexible wedge (a one-piece disk with a perimeter cut for flexibility, used where thermal expansion could otherwise clamp a solid gate shut — as in steam systems), split wedge (self-adjusting/self-aligning ball-and-socket design, suited to corrosive liquids and noncondensing gases), and parallel disk (designed to prevent binding from thermal transients, used in low and high pressure applications). Gate valves are classified as rising-stem or nonrising-stem, and seats are provided integral with the body or as a separate (often replaceable) seat ring.

### Globe Valves

A globe valve is a linear-motion valve used to stop, start, and regulate flow. The disk moves perpendicular to the seat, gradually closing the annular space between disk and seat ring as the valve closes — giving globe valves good throttling ability and generally much less seat leakage than gate valves (the disk-to-seat contact is closer to a right angle, permitting a tighter seal). Drawbacks include high head loss from the two or more right-angle turns in the flow path, and (in large, high-pressure applications) fluid dynamic effects that can damage trim, packing, and actuators, plus greater weight and noise. The three primary body designs are **Z-body** (simplest, most common for water, stem/disk travel at right angles to the pipe axis), **Y-body** (seat and stem angled ~45°, remedying the high pressure drop of the Z-body, best suited to high pressure/severe service), and **angle** (a simple modification with ends at right angles, doubling as a valve and a piping elbow). Disk designs include ball disks (low pressure/temperature, mainly stop-start), composition disks (a hard nonmetallic insert, used in steam/hot water, erosion-resistant, replaceable), and plug disks (long and tapered, providing better throttling than ball or composition designs). Many globe valves have a backseat that seals between stem and bonnet when the valve is fully open, protecting the packing from system pressure.

### Ball Valves

A ball valve is a rotational-motion valve using a ball-shaped disk with a bored hole: turning the handle 90° aligns the hole with the body's inlet/outlet to open the valve, or rotates it perpendicular to stop flow. Most actuators are quick-acting (90° turn); others are planetary-gear-operated for mechanical advantage on larger valves. Ball valves are generally the least expensive configuration, have low maintenance costs, require no lubrication, and give tight sealing with low torque — but conventional ball valves have relatively poor throttling characteristics (the partially exposed seat erodes rapidly under high-velocity throttling flow). They are available in venturi, reduced, and full port patterns (full port = bore equal to the pipe's inside diameter); seats are typically elastomeric (TFE, filled TFE, Nylon, Buna-N, Neoprene), which limits use at elevated temperatures.

### Plug Valves

A plug valve is a rotational-motion valve whose disk is a solid plug with a bored passage; turning the plug 90° from open blocks the ports and stops flow. Plug valves adapt easily to multiport construction, simplifying piping and potentially eliminating as many as four conventional shutoff valves in transfer/diverting service (though many multiport configurations do not achieve complete shutoff of all paths). They are normally used for on-off, non-throttling, frequent-operation service (a diamond-shaped port has been developed for throttling). Plug port shapes include rectangular (the most common, ≥70% of the pipe's cross-sectional area), round (full port if ≥pipe ID, else "standard round port"), and diamond (for throttling, always venturi-restricted). Plug valves are either lubricated (grease injected through the stem lubricates and seals the plug-to-body gap; common on gases and liquid hydrocarbons, up to 24 inches and 6000 psig) or nonlubricated (lift-type, which mechanically lifts the plug off its seat before rotating; or elastomer-sleeve/plug-coated, where a durable, self-lubricating TFE sleeve maintains the primary seal at all times).

### Diaphragm Valves

A diaphragm valve is a linear-motion valve used to start, regulate, and stop flow via a flexible disk (diaphragm) that mates with a seat at the top of the body — in effect a "pinch clamp" valve. The compressor, driven by the valve stem, presses the diaphragm against a contoured bottom (straight-through type) or a body weir (weir type); the weir type is the better throttling valve, though with a limited range. Because the operating mechanism is never exposed to the process fluid, diaphragm valves are well suited to corrosive fluids, fibrous slurries, radioactive fluids, or other fluids that must stay free of contamination — there are no packing glands to maintain and no possibility of stem leakage. Diaphragm material choice depends on the fluid, temperature, pressure, and operating frequency; most elastomeric materials perform best below 150°F, though some (e.g. Viton) tolerate higher temperatures at the cost of reduced tensile and bond strength. Larger diaphragm valves (4 inches and up) intended for hazardous or vacuum service typically use a sealed, evacuated bonnet to guard against premature diaphragm failure and to contain any release if the diaphragm does fail.

### Reducing Valves

A reducing valve automatically reduces supply pressure to a preselected downstream pressure as long as supply pressure remains at least as high as the setpoint. In a **variable** reducing valve, high inlet pressure and a main valve spring keep the main valve closed while some high pressure is bled to an auxiliary valve controlling a piston atop the main valve disk (which has more surface area than the disk, producing a net opening force); the auxiliary valve is itself positioned by a controlling diaphragm balancing an adjusting-spring force (set by an adjusting screw) against reduced outlet pressure bled back beneath the diaphragm. A **non-variable** reducing valve replaces the adjusting spring/screw with a pre-pressurized dome over the diaphragm and eliminates the intermediate auxiliary valve, reacting the opposing forces directly on the diaphragm — making it more responsive to large pressure swings and less failure-prone than the variable design.

### Pinch Valves

The pinch valve is one of the simplest valve designs — essentially an industrial version of a laboratory pinch cock, consisting of a molded rubber (or synthetic) sleeve, referred to as the valve body, and an external pinching mechanism completely isolated from the fluid. Suitable for on-off and throttling service (effective throttling range roughly 10%-95% of rated capacity), pinch valves are well suited to slurries, liquids with large amounts of suspended solids, and pneumatically conveyed solids, and to services where metal contamination or corrosion from a conventional valve body would be a problem. Typical maximum operating temperature is 250°F, with maximum pressure ranging from about 100 psig (1-inch valve) down to 15 psig (12-inch valve) at that temperature; special versions handle -100°F to 550°F and up to 300 psig. Exposed-sleeve designs have limited vacuum applicability (the sleeve tends to collapse); fully encased designs can be used in vacuum service by applying vacuum within the metal casing.

### Butterfly Valves

A butterfly valve is a rotary-motion valve used to stop, regulate, and start flow via a disk approximately the diameter of the adjoining pipe, rotating on a vertical or horizontal axis — a 90° handle rotation moves the disk from fully closed to fully open. Compared to gate, globe, plug, and ball valves, butterfly valves offer savings in weight, space, and cost (especially at large sizes), low maintenance (few moving parts, no fluid-trapping pockets), and suitability for large flows of liquids or gases at relatively low pressure, or for slurries/liquids with suspended solids. Sealing is against an elastomeric seat or a seal-ring arrangement (early designs used metal-to-metal seating, which was not leak-tight but adequate for some services such as water distribution). Body construction ranges from the economical wafer type (fits between two pipe flanges) to lug-wafer, flanged-end, and threaded-end designs.

### Needle Valves

A needle valve makes relatively fine adjustments to flow using a long, tapered, needle-like stem point as the disk; because the tapered part is smaller than the seat orifice and passes through it before seating, opening/closing is very gradual. Needle valves are often used as components within other valves (e.g., some reducing valves) and in constant-pressure pump governors and precise automatic combustion-control components. A common body design is the bar-stock body. Needle valves are frequently used as metering valves for extremely fine flow control (a typical metering valve stem has 40 threads per inch, giving a direct relationship between handwheel turns and flow) and are often equipped with replaceable seats.

### Check Valves

Check valves prevent reversal of flow in a piping system, activated by the flowing fluid itself — forward flow opens the valve, reversal closes it (by the weight of the check mechanism, back pressure, a spring, or a combination). General types are swing, tilting-disk, lift, piston, butterfly, and stop check valves.

- **Swing check valves** allow full, unobstructed flow with very low turbulence/pressure drop, and are normally paired with gate valves for that reason; the disk and hinge are suspended from the body by a hinge pin, with metal-to-metal or metal-to-composition-disk seating. They are recommended for low-velocity, non-pulsating flow (pulsating flow can cause destructive flapping).
- **Tilting disk check valves** are similar to swing checks with low resistance/turbulence from a straight-through design; the airfoil-shaped disk "floats" on the flow and seals before reverse flow occurs, moving across a soft seal into a metal seat for tight shutoff without slamming.
- **Lift check valves** have seating arrangements similar to globe valves and are commonly paired with them; flow must always enter below the seat, lifting the disk/ball off the seat, which reseats under backflow and gravity when flow stops or reverses. Available in horizontal, angle, and vertical body patterns.
- **Piston check valves** are essentially lift check valves with a dashpot (piston-and-cylinder) that provides a cushioning effect; used mainly with globe and angle valves in systems with frequent flow-direction changes.
- **Butterfly check valves** share a seating arrangement and flow characteristics with butterfly valves; a flexible sealing member seats against the body bore at a 45° angle, and the short full-open-to-full-closed travel inhibits the slamming seen in some other check-valve types.
- **Stop check valves** combine a lift check valve and a globe valve: a stem, when closed, prevents the disk from leaving the seat and provides a tight globe-valve-like seal; when the stem is opened, the valve operates as a plain lift check (the stem is not connected to the disk).

### Relief and Safety Valves

Relief and safety valves prevent equipment damage by relieving accidental overpressurization. A **relief valve** gradually opens as inlet pressure rises above its setpoint, opening only as much as necessary to relieve the overpressure — typically used on incompressible fluids such as water or oil. A **safety valve** pops rapidly to full open as soon as the pressure setting is reached and stays open until pressure drops below a lower reset pressure; the difference between the actuating setpoint and the reset pressure is called **blowdown**, expressed as a percentage of the setpoint — typically used on compressible fluids such as steam or other gases, and often identifiable by an external lever used for an operational check. Most relief and safety valves are held closed by a compression spring whose compression (and thus the pressure setpoint) is adjusted via nuts on the yoke; system pressure opens the valve once it overcomes the spring force. **Pilot-operated relief valves** use a small passage to the top of a piston connected to the stem, so system pressure normally holds the main valve closed; when a small (often solenoid-operated) pilot valve opens — signaled by a pressure-measuring system — pressure is relieved from the piston and system pressure opens the main valve.

## Valve Actuators

Actuators position a valve and range from simple manual handwheels to complex electrical and hydraulic manipulators; selection depends on required torque and whether automatic actuation is needed. All actuator types except manual handwheel/lever are adaptable to automatic actuation.

- **Manual, fixed, and hammer actuators** — the handwheel is the most common manual actuator. Handwheels fixed directly to the stem give only the mechanical advantage of the wheel itself (and can bind at high temperature); hammer handwheels turn freely through part of a rotation before striking a lug on a secondary wheel attached to the stem, allowing the valve to be pounded shut or open; gear-operated handwheels add mechanical advantage for large valves at the cost of longer operating time (portable air motors can be connected to the gear pinion to speed this up).
- **Electric motor actuators** permit manual, semi-automatic, and automatic operation, mostly for open-close functions though adaptable to positioning at any point of travel. A reversible, high-speed motor connects through a gear train that reduces speed and increases torque at the stem; limit switches (position- or torque-operated) normally stop the motor automatically at full open and full closed.
- **Pneumatic actuators** translate an air signal into stem motion via air pressure on a diaphragm or piston, used where fast action is required. An actuator is "direct-acting" if air pressure closes the valve and a spring opens it, or "reverse-acting" if air pressure opens it and a spring closes it; duplex actuators supply air to both sides of the diaphragm, positioning the stem via the differential pressure.
- **Hydraulic actuators** work similarly to pneumatic actuators but use a piston with hydraulic fluid (water or oil) fed to one side while the other drains; solenoid or manual valves direct the hydraulic fluid for automatic or semi-automatic operation.
- **Self-actuated valves** (relief valves, safety valves, check valves, steam traps) use some characteristic of the system fluid itself to actuate — no external power source is needed.
- **Solenoid actuated valves** position the valve by attracting a magnetic slug attached to the stem, typically opposed by a spring; most have a manual override. Single-solenoid valves are termed fail-open or fail-closed based on their de-energized position; double-solenoid valves typically fail "as is" (no position change when both solenoids are de-energized). A common application is controlling the air supply to a pneumatic valve actuator.
- **Speed of power actuators** — plant safety considerations dictate required valve speeds: some safety-related valves need very fast actuation (rapid isolation), while others need slower opening to avoid thermal shock (e.g., injecting cold water into a hot system). Hydraulic, pneumatic, and solenoid actuators generally provide the fastest actuation (solenoids are impractical at large valve sizes); electric motor actuation speed is set by motor speed and gear ratio, typically achieving full travel in roughly two to several seconds.
- **Valve position indication** — remote indication uses position lights driven by a position detector (a mechanical limit switch, or a magnetic switch/transformer sensing a magnetic core) sensing stem, disk, or actuator position. Local indication is provided by visible stem position (rising-stem valves) or a mechanical pointer operated by the actuator (nonrising-stem or power-actuated valves); some valves have no position-indication feature at all.
